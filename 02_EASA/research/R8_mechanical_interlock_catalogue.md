## Precedent findings

### P1. Trapped-key interlocks (industry)

1. **Principle.** The system "uses a coded sequence of keys and locks to enforce a predetermined order of operations". "The key is
   always trapped in either the isolation point or the access point." [D-ag, S5]
2. **Exchange box.** A free key goes into an exchange box; "the access key(s) are now released, and the free key is trapped". [D-ag, S5]
   — So one key's release proves one condition (e.g. power off) and that proof travels to the next step. [Y]
3. **Standard.** ISO 14119:2024 has a normative **Annex K "Trapped key interlocking systems"**, a clause **8.4 "Additional measures
   to minimize possibility of defeat for Type 5 devices"** with **8.4.2 Key retention** and **8.4.3 Reproduction of keys**, clause 8
   "Design to minimize the motivation to defeat", and an informative **Annex G "Motivation to defeat interlocking devices"**. [D-ag, S3 ToC]
   - Trapped-key systems "previously covered by ISO/TS 19837:2018 ... have been integrated in Annex K and have been classified as
     type 5 interlocking devices". "Key coding aims to prevent two or more interlocking devices from unintentionally sharing an
     identical key". "Keys must not be easily reproducible". [D-ag, S4]
   - **Correction to R7 item 25:** in the 2024 edition the motivation-to-defeat annex is **Annex G**; Annex H is "Examples for maximum
     static action forces". [D-ag, S3]
   - The clause text itself was not read (paywalled). [NOT VERIFIED]
4. **How it is defeated: the spare key.** CPM Group (UK, 2016, fatal): access "was controlled by a key system that should have made sure
   machinery was isolated"; after the event "a spare key was discovered which meant that access to the machinery in the incident area
   was possible". Fine £660,000. [D-ag, S6]
   - Other HSE cases (spare key kept on top of the machine; supervisors each given an override key) were seen in search snippets only. [Y]
5. **How it fails** [Y, general engineering; not read at a source this session]: key or lock jams (dirt, ice, wear); key breaks in the
   lock; a lost key stops the process (so people make spares, which is the defeat route in item 4).

### P2. Aircraft lock, flag and vent-door precedents

6. **Cessna control lock covers the ignition switch.** "The lock consists of a shaped steel rod and flag. The flag identifies the control
   lock and cautions about its removal before starting the engine." "Proper installation of the lock will place the flag over the
   ignition switch." [D-ag, S7 p. 7-18] — This is the team lead's seed idea, in production since at least 1996. [Y]
7. **…and it still fails.** A search snippet describes NTSB WPR17CA001 (Cessna R172K): the lock was installed **backwards**, so it did
   not block the ignition switch, and the pilot took off with it in place. A 2026 news item describes a lock that **no longer covered the
   switch after an avionics change**. [Y — snippets only; ASN and General Aviation News returned 403/DNS errors; NTSB report not found]
8. **Beech moved the lock hole so a fitted lock is obvious.** Early King Air control-wheel locks left "the control wheel ... level with the
   lock pin installed". "After some reports of accidents caused by the control lock still being installed at takeoff", Beech put "the
   hole in the control wheel shaft in a different position". [D-ag, S8 p. 20]
   - Beech SB 27-3459 (2002, Rev 1 Nov 2016) warns that accidents followed "the failure of a pilot to remove an unauthorized or altered
     flight control (gust) lock"; sometimes "a common bolt or nail had been inserted through the hole". [D-ag, S9]
   - Lesson [Y]: make the unsafe state **visibly abnormal**, and expect home-made substitutes that lack the warning feature.
9. **Gust-lock rule and the G-IV**: already in R7 items 13–20 (CS 23.679 "unmistakable warning"; throttle-limiting accepted by the FAA;
   G-IV interlock allowed 18–24° instead of 6° through tolerance stack; shown by drawing review only). Not redone.
10. **Gear warning: a throttle stop is not accepted as the warning.** CS 23.729(f)(1): "A throttle stop may not be used in place of an
    aural device." [D-ag, S2 p. 160] — This is about **closing** the throttle on approach with the gear up; a stop there would block a
    go-around/descent need. It shows regulators distrust a stop as the *only* warning. [Y]
11. **Mechanical coupling of the indicator must not lie.** CS 23.729(e): gear indicator switches "must be located and coupled to the
    landing gear mechanical system in a manner that prevents an erroneous indication of either 'down and locked' if each gear is not in
    the fully extended position". [D-ag, S2 p. 160]
12. **Doors forward of the propeller (CS 23.783(e)):** direct visual inspection of the locking mechanism, and a visual warning designed so that
    an erroneous "closed and locked" indication is improbable. [D-ag, S2 p. 171] (Already noted by R3; quoted here because ideas C and D
    are "direct visual inspection" means.)
13. **The "can't-pressurise-unless-locked" rule (CS-25 transport).** 14 CFR 25.783(c): "There must be a provision to prevent pressurization
    of the airplane to an unsafe level" if a door is not fully closed, latched and locked; it must work "after any single failure".
    (d)(5): "It must not be possible to position the lock in the locked position if the latch and the latching mechanism are not in the
    latched position." [D-ag, S10]
14. **DC-10 Windsor 1972: the vent-door interlock was beaten by force.** The agent "had to force the handle closed with his knee". In tests
    at Douglas the vent door could be closed and the handle stowed **without the lock-pins engaged** with about **120 lb** on the handle,
    through "deflection of the operating mechanisms and tolerances". The bent lock tube touched the cockpit warning switch arm, which
    "can be actuated with less than one-eighth inch travel", giving a "door safe" light. [D-ag, S11 pp. 8–9, App. B]
    - NTSB A-72-97: make it "physically impossible to position the external locking handle and vent door to their normal door locked
      positions unless the locking pins are fully engaged". [D-ag, S11 App. B]
15. **B747 UAL811 1989: the flag was linked to the handle linkage, not to the latch cams.** The lock-sector position "is indicated
    indirectly by noting visually the closed position of the two pressure relief doors". These doors "are mechanically linked to the
    movement of the lock sectors". A switch on one relief door turns off the cockpit light. [D-ag, S12 pp. 15–16]
    - Boeing's 1970 failure analysis: "Any failure ... which results in open latches will always be indicated by open pressure relief
      doors." The FAA approved this instead of using the eight cam **view ports**; view-port maintenance and checks were then dropped.
      [D-ag, S12 pp. 65–66]
    - NTSB: the assumption "was not valid": the cams could be back-driven open "with the master latch lock handle stowed, while a false
      indication was provided to the flightcrew". [D-ag, S12 p. 83]
    - UAL 747 cargo-door SDRs, 1983–1989: **9 cases of door-open (false) indications**. [D-ag, S12 p. 22] — nuisance history erodes trust. [Y]
16. **A320 fan cowl doors: 38 losses, passive fixes only "partially effective".** 34 losses before G-EUOE, 38 after. "In all cases, cowls were
    opened prior to the flight and were not correctly resecured." [D-ag, S13 pp. 70–71]
    - Fixes tried: high-visibility paint on latches; **anti-swivel plates and weights** "to make the latches hang down when not fully
      engaged"; a **hold-open device** "to prevent the fan cowl doors from closing fully when not latched and to provide a visible gap";
      a **"remove before flight" flag and pin** on the hold-open device. [D-ag, S13 pp. 73–75]
    - AAIB: these "have been only partially effective"; "procedurally-based safety actions are limited in their effectiveness"; the walk-round
      "is not a reliable barrier". SR 2015-002 asks for "a reliable means of warning when the fan cowl doors are unlatched". [D-ag, S13 pp. 92–94]
17. **A320 fan cowl "latch with key": a real aviation trapped key.** "A new FCD front latch and keeper assembly ... having a specific key
    necessary to un-latch the FCD. This key cannot be removed unless the FCD front latch is safely closed. The key, after removal, must be
    stowed in the flight deck at a specific location". [D-ag, S15 MCAI quote; same words in S14 and S16]
    - The AAIB report (2015) described the study as "a mechanical lock and key (with streamer flag)" where "when the latch is open, the key
      cannot be removed and is visible with the streamer flag below the latch". [D-ag, S13 p. 94]
    - Mandated: EASA AD 2016-0053 → FAA AD 2017-13-10 (V2500); EASA AD 2016-0069/2016-0257 (CFM56). [D-ag for the cross-references in S15;
      the EASA AD site showed a maintenance page, so the EASA ADs were not read]
    - **Missing-key dispatch rule:** "Flights with one or both FCD keys missing from the stowage location in the fight deck ... are permitted
      for a period not to exceed 10 calendar days". [D-ag, S15 para. (j)] — The regulator planned for lost keys. [Y]
    - Operators objected to the flight-deck placard; FAA kept it "to ensure that the key is stowed in a particular location onboard". [D-ag, S14]
    - Cost: $4,865 parts + 7 h labour = $5,460 per aircraft (CFM56 NPRM). [D-ag, S16]
18. **Europa XS: an external stop that makes the handle honest.** The door lever could reach "closed" with the rear tapered shoot bolt
    "resting against the outside of the fuselage skin". LAA SM 15833 (mandatory, Nov 2021) adds "an aerodynamically shaped stop" that
    "blocks the rear latch pin's movement" in that case, plus "brightly coloured warning marks" on the handles. At least 6 of 8 events were
    the pilot's own (left) door, which is harder for him to check. [D-ag, S18 pp. 44–46]
19. **ASK 21 front/rear canopy interlock** and its bypass; **Standard Cirrus optical indicator (AD 2020-0260)**: see R7_visual §4.1. Not redone.
20. **Ejection-seat pins.** A US spec (MIL-S-18471G) is said to require pin stowage "accessible to a seated aircrewmember" for "safety pins and
    streamers". [Y — search snippet; the host failed a TLS check] — Precedent for "the token's stowage proves the step". [Y]

### P3. Mass-market mechanical/key interlocks

21. **Cars (FMVSS 114).** Key removal is prevented "unless the transmission or gear selection control is locked in 'park'". The selector
    "cannot move from the 'park' position ... unless the key is in the starting system". The brake must be pressed before leaving park.
    [D-ag, S17 S5.2.1, S5.2.2, S5.3]
    - Overrides allowed: key release "in the event of electrical failure"; or a device needing "the use of a tool", or one behind "an opaque
      surface" removable "only by using a screwdriver or other tool". [D-ag, S17 S5.2.3–S5.2.4]
    - Lesson [Y]: an interlock fitted to millions of vehicles survives because it never blocks normal use, and its override needs a **tool
      and a deliberate act**, not force.

### P4. Extra NG facts that decide the ideas

22. **The linkage.** "The three shoot bolts are attached to a linkage tube which is held in the locked position by a tension spring." The
    handle "protrudes through the canopy frame". [D-ag, S1 p. 8]
23. **The 10 mm that matters.** Released handle "with either the canopy open or correctly locked shut": 70 mm between handle centres.
    Closed and released under spring alone: stops "just as it enters the green lock area" at **60 mm** (partial lock). Full lock needs a
    manual pull to 70 mm with a "distinct 'clunk'". [D-ag, S1 pp. 8–9]
    - **Consequence [Y]:** anything driven by the linkage tube or handle reads **the same at "canopy open, handle released" and at "locked"**.
      Any tube-driven interlock is satisfied by an open canopy. It must be combined with a "canopy seated" input, or sense at the lugs.
24. **Hidden bolts.** In normal circumstances the shoot bolt and latch engagement "are completely hidden from view". There are "no observable
    visual differences" at the frame edge between full and partial lock. [D-ag, S1 pp. 9, 20]
25. **Forces.** Open from full lock: 136 N. Partial → full: **60 N**. Open from partial: 90 N. Driving partial-locked bolts open by air loads:
    at least 270 N. [D-ag, S1 pp. 20, 24] — Any add-on spring that resists the last 10 mm of bolt travel makes partial lock **more** likely.
    Keep add-on resisting forces to a few newtons. [Y]
26. **Jettison and egress.** Emergency opening "is equal to the normal procedure"; jettison: "Push canopy slightly forward while opening." [D-ag,
    S1 p. 11] The pilot wears a parachute (G-MIIL pilot did, S1 p. 1). [D-ag] — Nothing may lock the canopy shut. [Y]
27. **Where things are.** The G-MIIL pilot "flew with his left hand permanently on the throttle" (throttle on the left) [D-ag, S1 p. 24]. He
    "closed the canopy, before completing the pre-start checks and starting the engine" [D-ag, S1 pp. 1–2] — one pilot, one flight.
    The bolts and handle are on the canopy (moves, jettisons); the lugs with rollers are on the fuselage side (R1 note, PKBWL p. 6). [Y from R1]
28. **Cable lost motion.** Backlash "is a function of the clearance between the core and liner, the input force, and the total number of degrees
    of bend". Maximum backlash for 360° of bend: about **3.1–5.8 mm** depending on series (the extracted table is mis-aligned; values are
    the column as printed). Lost motion (backlash plus stretch) "will increase as cable length, degrees of bend, and loads are increased".
    [D-ag, S19 printed p. 5] — Against a 10 mm partial-to-full signal, a routed Bowden cable can eat 30–60 % of the margin. [Y]

## Idea catalogue (one card per idea)

Common reading of the cards [Y]:
- "Senses" says what physical state drives the device: **handle/tube** (fooled by open-canopy-released, item 23) or **bolt-in-lug** (truth).
- "Scope" = in/out of the team rule (no change to how the latch locks).
- Mass and effort are R8 estimates, not measured.

### Card A1 — Seed idea, safe form: start-button guard held shut until all bolts are home

- **How it works.** A spring-closed flap over the starter button (or over the START detent of a key/mag switch). A cable or rod lets it open
  only when all three bolts are fully home **and** the canopy is seated. Once the engine runs, nothing more happens.
- **Senses.** Should be bolt-in-lug via fuselage-side plungers (Card D). If driven from the tube, an open canopy with the handle released also
  opens the flap (item 23).
- **Where on the Extra NG.** Rear panel or left console at the starter. The starter type and position are not known (POH not obtained).
- **Nuisance.** Blocks **starting** with the canopy open. If Extra pilots start with the canopy open in heat, this is the seat-belt-interlock
  nuisance pattern (R7 items 26–27). The G-MIIL pilot closed it before start (item 27) — one case only. [NOT VERIFIED for the fleet]
- **Benefit limit.** Proves "locked at start" only. Opening the canopy for taxi cooling and re-closing to a partial lock is not caught.
- **Failure modes.** Cable stretch → flap opens at partial lock (false permit); jam → cannot start (safe, a no-go on the ground); flap removed
  or bent open (Cessna lock fitted backwards, item 7).
- **Must never** cover the OFF direction of the mag/ignition switch or the mixture cut-off (shut-down must stay free). [Y]
- **Scope.** In scope (adds a guard; latch unchanged).
- **Mass/complexity.** ~50–150 g; low–medium; cable routing from three lugs is the hard part. [Y]
- **Precedent.** Cessna flag over the ignition switch (item 6); car key/park interlock (item 21); A320 key (item 17).

### Card A2 — Seed idea, literal form: "flap won't open unless locked; once the key is in, the flap can't close"

- **Reading 1 (key blocks the flap only).** The key holds the flap open; the cable from the canopy side has lost-motion/spring so the canopy can
  still be unlocked. Then the device is A1 plus nothing: unlocking after start gives no signal. Could add a spring "tell": if the canopy is
  unlocked while the key holds the flap, a red flag pops at the flap. That turns it into a weak in-cockpit indicator (Card C behaviour). [Y]
- **Reading 2 (key in → canopy linkage blocked = classic trapped key).** The canopy could not be unlocked while the key is in. This **adds a lock
  to the latch**, blocks egress after a crash or fire, and blocks jettison/bail-out in flight (item 26). **Out of scope and unsafe. Reject.**
  Industrial trapped keys protect people *from* the machine; here the "machine" is the escape path. [Y]
- **Scope.** Reading 1 in scope; Reading 2 out of scope.
- **Precedent.** Castell exchange logic (items 1–2); A320 key (one-shot proof, item 17).

### Card A3 — Key-exchange token: "canopy key" released only by a locked canopy, needed to start

- **How it works.** A small coded lock on the fuselage sill next to a lug. Its probe can rotate (and free the key) only if the bolt is fully home.
  The key then unlocks the starter guard (A1). Like the A320 key that "cannot be removed unless the ... latch is safely closed" (item 17).
- **Senses.** Bolt-in-lug at one lug (or three probes on one rod). Truth-based, if the probe reads the last 2–3 mm of bolt travel.
- **Important.** The probe must **withdraw** after the key leaves, or it would lock the bolt (Reading 2 problem). [Y]
- **Nuisance/benefit.** As A1, plus a key to lose. The A320 rule allows 10 days with a missing key (item 17): plan a dispatch rule. Spare keys
  are the classic defeat (item 4).
- **Scope.** In scope if the probe never holds the bolt. Medium complexity, ~100–200 g. [Y]

### Card B — Throttle blocking pawl driven by the bolts (mechanical take-off gate)

- **How it works.** A spring pawl in the throttle quadrant limits forward travel to about run-up power. A push-pull cable from the lug plungers
  (Card D) lifts the pawl when all three bolts are home. Taxi and run-up stay possible with the canopy open (R7 implication 7).
- **Senses.** Bolt-in-lug (if fed from Card D). From the tube it would be fooled by an open canopy (item 23).
- **Where.** Left-side throttle quadrant (item 27). Cable from the left sill lugs, short, few bends.
- **Strength.** The FAA calls a physical limit "an unmistakable warning" (R7 item 14). It acts at the moment that matters (take-off power).
- **Weaknesses.**
  - **No ground/air logic.** Pure mechanics cannot tell ground from air. If a bolt backs off in flight, or the cable jams, the pawl could stop a
    re-advance after a power reduction: a **partial power limit in flight**, the worst kind (R7 item 33). CS 23.679(c) principle: must not
    engage in flight. Needs a **breakout** by a separate, distinct action (CS 23.1155 pattern, R7 item 15) and a latch-out once passed. [Y]
  - **Tolerance stack.** G-IV: a designed 6° became 18–24° in service (R7 item 16). Cable lost motion of 3–6 mm (item 28) versus a 10 mm signal.
    Needs a functional test at each annual and a rig check with the 60 mm partial-lock state as the test case. [Y]
  - **Force defeat.** DC-10 (120 lb, item 14), G-IV (lock pin failed, R7 item 18). Pilots push through an unexplained stop. Show the reason
    ("CANOPY") on the pawl. [Y]
  - Regulators reject a stop as the only gear warning (item 10): pair it with a flag or alert. [Y]
- **Scope.** In scope (no change to the latch). **Mass/complexity:** ~150–300 g; medium–high; needs throttle-quadrant work. [Y]
- **Precedent.** G-IV gust-lock throttle limiter (R7 items 16–17); CS 23.679 (R7 item 13).

### Card C — Mechanical flag in the pilot's view, driven by the linkage tube

- **How it works.** On the canopy frame near the rear handle (both move together, so no cable crosses the hinge). A lever multiplies the tube's
  last 10 mm (e.g. 3:1 → 30 mm). A red/white flag shows "NOT LOCKED" until the last ~2 mm; green "LOCKED" only at the end stop.
- **Senses.** Handle/tube. It **does** separate partial (60 mm) from full (70 mm), which is the Extra's known failure mode (item 23, R1 SB step 19).
  It does **not** prove the canopy is seated in the lugs (BD-10/747 lesson, items 15, R7 sensing §5).
- **Where.** Left canopy rail, rear cockpit, within the pilot's normal glance; a repeater for the front handle is not needed (same tube). [Y]
- **Nuisance.** None (it only shows). Open canopy shows green too, but then the open canopy is obvious.
- **Failure modes.** Flag sticks green (false safe) → make the flag swing red under its own spring and check it goes red **every time the handle
  is squeezed** (a built-in self-test each flight). Lever wear. [Y]
- **Limits.** Passive: the A320 paint/flags were "only partially effective" (item 16). Must add only ~1 N to the tube (item 25). [Y]
- **Scope.** In scope (adds a part; latch locks the same way). ~30–60 g; low effort. [Y]
- **Precedent.** Standard Cirrus optical indicator AD 2020-0260 (R7); Europa handle marks (item 18); 747 relief doors as the warning case (item 15).

### Card D — Bolt-end tell-tales (pop-up pins) at each roller lug

- **How it works.** On the fuselage-side fitting, beyond the roller, a light plunger sits in the bolt's path. Only the last 2–3 mm of bolt travel
  (roller on the top flat) push it. It moves a small flag from red to hidden (or green). Spring returns it to red when the bolt leaves.
- **Senses.** Bolt-in-lug. This is the "direct visual inspection of the locking mechanism" of CS 23.783(e)(2) (item 12), and the lesson of the
  747 view ports (item 15) and DC-10 A-72-97 (item 14): look at the pins, not the handle.
- **Where.** Three lugs on the left cockpit sill. The rear-seat pilot can probably see the rear one; the front one is hard to see. [Y]
- **Second job.** The same plungers are the best **mechanical source** for Cards A1, A3 and B, and for a microswitch feeding the alert (R7).
- **Nuisance.** None.
- **Failure modes.** Plunger stuck "home" (corrosion, ice, foam/sealant — SB-NG-2-22 warns foam can block the latch, R0 note) → false safe. Test:
  must show red every time the canopy is opened. Plunger spring too stiff → resists the last bolt travel → **more partial locks** (item 25).
  Keep it ≤ ~3 N. Misrigging, like the RV-14 switch (R7 sensing §5). [Y]
- **Scope.** In scope only if the plunger does not touch the lock geometry or carry load; it is an add-on bracket on the fitting. Borderline:
  confirm with the team. [Y]
- **Mass/complexity.** ~10–20 g per lug; medium (fitting space unknown; POH/AMM not obtained). [Y]
- **Precedent.** A320 anti-swivel latches that "hang down when not fully engaged" (item 16); 747 cam view ports (item 15); BD-10 match marks (R7).

### Card E — Mixture or fuel-valve interlock

- **Mixture:** block the mixture leaving idle cut-off until locked. Same effect as A1 (start inhibit), but the mixture is also the normal
  shut-down control; a jam near the cut-off end is a real hazard. Worse than A1. [Y]
- **Fuel valve:** forcing the valve OFF gives a **delayed engine stop**, maybe at lift-off. That is R7 option E (in-flight power loss). **Reject.**
- **Scope.** In scope technically; not recommended.

### Card F — Checklist holder and harness-buckle interlocks

- **Harness buckle locked until canopy locked:** wrong order. The pilot straps in before closing (G-MIIL, item 27). Blocking or delaying harness
  release is an egress hazard. **Reject.** [Y]
- **"Canopy tag" on the throttle (low-tech token).** A red tag on a short lanyard is clipped over the throttle knob at shut-down. It can only be
  parked in a clip on the rear handle bracket whose slot lines up **only at the 70 mm full-lock position**. Cheap, no cable, a physical
  memory aid at the right moment. Defeat: park it anywhere else. Fooled by an open canopy (tube-based). [Y, R8 idea; no precedent beyond
  the Cessna flag (item 6) and seat-pin streamers (item 20)]
- **Scope.** In scope. ~20 g. Very low effort.

### Card G — Stronger: hybrid "one truth" — bolt plungers drive both a flag and a switch

- **How it works.** Card D plungers carry (1) the visible tell-tale and (2) a switch/proximity target for the electric alert and gate in R7.
  The pilot can confirm an alert by looking at the same element the sensor reads. That lowers cry-wolf cost (R7_visual implication 8).
- **Why stronger.** Every mechanical-only interlock above has either no ground/air logic (B) or acts only at start (A1/A3). The electric gate in
  R7 has the logic but can lie if its switch reads the handle (G-IIDY, RV-14, BD-10). Taking the signal at the bolt fixes the lie;
  the electronics add take-off arming, flash/aural and self-test. [Y]
- **Scope.** In scope. Mass ~50–100 g on top of R7's system. Medium effort.
- **Precedent.** 25.783(e) separate "latched" vs "locked" sensing (R3); CS 23.729(e) coupling rule (item 11); AAIB SR 2015-002 asking for a
  *reliable warning* after passive fixes failed (item 16).

### Card H — Handle travel gauge (marking only)

- A narrow green band visible only at the 70 mm end stop, and red/yellow at 60 mm, printed on a small scale beside the rear handle. The AAIB
  found the partial lock "just enters the green lock area" (item 23). Zero risk, grams, but passive and tube-based. Check against the AD
  2025-0156 placards first (not read by R8). [Y]

## Design implications [Y]

All items are R8 interpretation.

1. **Sense the bolt in the lug, not the handle or tube.** The Extra tube reads the same "open-released" and "locked" (item 23). DC-10, 747, BD-10,
   G-IIDY and RV-14 all show handle-side indication lying (items 14–15; R7). The fuselage-side lug is where the truth is, and it does not move
   or jettison with the canopy.
2. **Never add a lock.** No idea may stop the canopy being unlocked or jettisoned (item 26). That kills the classic trapped-key form (A2 reading 2).
   A key or flap may **prove** a step; it must not **hold** the bolt.
3. **Respect the 60 N.** Any spring or plunger in the bolt path must be a few newtons at most, or it pushes pilots into the partial lock (item 25).
4. **A cable has 3–6 mm of slack in a 10 mm signal** (items 23, 28). Prefer short rods or plungers at the lug; if a cable is used, keep it short and
   nearly straight, and rig/test with the 60 mm partial-lock state.
5. **A start-only interlock catches only "unlocked at start".** It is weak if pilots open the canopy for taxi. The take-off-power moment is where a
   physical stop pays (Card B), but a purely mechanical pawl has no ground/air logic: give it a distinct release action and a latch-out, or use
   the electric gate of R7 fed by the bolt plungers (Card G).
6. **Plan for defeat and loss.** Spare keys (item 4), bent flags (item 7), home-made locks (item 8), force (item 14). Make the override a distinct
   act with a tool or cover (item 21), log it if electric, and allow a short, rule-based dispatch with a missing key (item 17).
7. **Self-test every flight.** Each passive tell-tale must visibly go red when the handle is squeezed or the canopy opens. A flag that never
   changes is a flag nobody reads.
8. **Passive alone is not enough.** A320: paint, gravity tell-tales, gap device and streamer flag were "only partially effective"; the AAIB asked
   for a reliable warning (item 16). Use mechanical tell-tales as the cross-check for the alert, not as the whole answer.
9. **For the pitch:** the strongest storyline is "**one truth at the bolt**" — the same plunger the pilot can see also drives the alert and the gate.
