## 1. Regulation and certification guidance: what "locked" indication must do

1. **AC 25.783-1A §12c, pp. 14–15 [D-ag, A1].** The flight deck must show if any door is not fully closed, latched **and locked**.
   "The door closed, latched, and locked functions must be monitored, but only one indicator is needed". The probability of an
   erroneous closed-latched-locked indication "must be no greater than 10-5" for doors that could be a hazard if unlatched.
2. **AC 25.783-1A §12b(1), p. 14 [D-ag, A1].** At the operator's station, "A single indication that directly monitors the door in the
   closed, latched, and locked conditions should be provided". A vent door or light "that monitors the door locks" may be enough.
3. **AC 25.783-1A §12b(3), p. 14 [D-ag, A1].** A passenger door with "shoot bolts" that extend and stop the door from closing far
   "would typically not need" an extra closed-before-latching feature. **Relevance [Y]:** the Extra NG shoot bolts do not stop the canopy
   closing. So a "closed" signal does not imply "bolted". Closed and locked must be sensed separately.
4. **AC 25.783-1A §13a(2), p. 16 [D-ag, A1].** The visual inspection provision must show "whether or not each latch is latched and each
   lock is in the locked position". Indirect viewing (flags, optics) is acceptable only if "there is no failure mode that could allow a
   false latched or locked indication". It must "Preclude mis-assembly that could result in a false latched and locked indication".
5. **AC 25.783-1A §6c, p. 4 [D-ag, A1].** "in some cases, the flight deck indication system has not been reliable. In other instances,
   the door indication system performed correctly, but the flightcrew … was not alerted". The 1989 cargo-door accidents (UAL811 and one
   other) led to the ATA study (1991) and Amendment 25-114 (2004).
6. **FAA directorate memo after the ATA Cargo Door Task Force, p. 2 [D-ag, A2].** For existing outward-opening doors:
   "(a) The Indication system must monitor the closed, latched, and locked positions, directly." "(c) Loss of indication or a false
   indication of a closed, latched and locked condition must be improbable." Where all locks sit on one lock shaft, viewing "the locks at
   each end may be sufficient … provided no failure condition in the lock shaft would go undetected". The memo covers A300, A310, A320,
   707/727, 737, 747, 757, 767, L-1011, DC-8, DC-9 and DC-10 cargo doors (p. 1). These criteria were copied into AD text for STC
   main-deck cargo doors (A6, 65 FR 41871 paras (a)–(d), 7 Jul 2000) [D-ag].
   - **Meaning for us [Y]:** "directly" is the post-UAL811/THY981 word. It rules out inferring "locked" from the handle or the drive.

## 2. Airliner and business-jet door lock sensing (field failures)

7. **Embraer EMB-545/-550, FAA AD 2025-13-02 (from ANAC AD 2024-08-02) [D-ag, A3 pp. 1–2, para (e)].** "a jamming failure of the main
   door lock sensor … could result in a false indication of a locked door, even when it is only latched, resulting in a dormant system
   failure and lack of cockpit indication of the door not locked condition." Fix: an operational test of the main door sensor at
   intervals ≤ **12 months**, lubrication of "the main door sensor mechanism" at ≤ **24 months**, and adjust or replace the sensor or its
   support (paras (g)–(h)). 296 US aircraft; up to 4 work-hours ($340) per test cycle (p. 2).
   - [Y] A sensor that needs its "mechanism" lubricated is mechanically actuated. The failure is **stuck in the locked state** and is
     **dormant**. This is the exact false-locked mode a periodic test must catch.
8. **Fokker 70/100, FAA AD 2009-18-11 [D-ag, A4, 74 FR 46334].** A false red "DOOR NOT LOCKED" light came on after lift-off. The cabin crew
   then operated the door lock handle. "This resulted in inadvertent opening of the downward opening passenger door in flight."
   Cause: wrong clearance between lever P/N A26997-003 and the **Up-Limit Switch**. The rigging limit was tightened from 1.3 ± 0.3 mm to
   **0.3 ± 0.2 mm** (AMM task 52-71-01-400-814-A). Cost about 4 work-hours per aircraft (NPRM 74 FR 27261).
   - [Y] Two lessons. (a) A microswitch on the handle lever is rigging-sensitive to a few tenths of a millimetre. (b) **A false
     "not locked" alarm in flight is itself a hazard:** people act on it and open the door. R8 found the same with in-flight canopy
     re-latching (4 reports).
9. **Boeing 757 off-wing slide compartment door, FAA AD 2012-01-09 [D-ag, A5, 77 FR 3579].** Required "relocating and adjusting the
   sensor target and the sensor proximity switch, and testing to ensure positive door locking", plus witness-mark placards.
   Prompted by in-flight loss of the slide. [Y] Proximity sensing is only as good as target placement and rigging.
10. **Airbus door patent US 5,480,109 [D-ag, P1].** Two sensors form one sensor unit per door. Two evaluation channels compare them.
    If the two signals disagree, the controller "automatically trips into a safety condition" (treats the door as not closed and locked).
    Door status also drives cabin pressure and air valves.
11. **Airbus passenger-door proximity sensors [Y].** Search snippets only (an A330/A340 training document on Scribd, a study-card site,
    an ATSB summary): the locking-handle sensor and the **locking-shaft** sensor together give "door closed" to the controller; the ECAM
    door symbol is green when closed and locked and amber when not locked. Not read at source. See NOT VERIFIED.
12. **Honeywell IHM aerospace inductive sensors, application note 003713-1-EN, pp. 1–2 [D-ag, H1].** Listed door uses: "Open or closed
    position" and "Locked status: Used to provide an additional security layer, meaning that the lock is in place, the proximity sensor
    reads Near". Also "Cargo loading latch detection (pallet locked)", thrust-reverser primary door locks, fan cowl doors, gear uplocks.
    Output is "three-state": target near, far, **fault detected**. "Non-contact design … regardless of the dirty, harsh environment".
    - [Y] Industry already uses one inductive sensor at the lock element itself, with built-in health monitoring. That is the team's
      chosen architecture, and the Honeywell note is a direct product precedent.
13. **747 UAL811 and DC-10 THY981 / AAR-73-02.** Already in `R8_mechanical_interlock_catalogue.md` items 14–15 (indication taken from the
    handle/linkage, not from the latch cams). The memo in item 6 and Amendment 25-114 are the regulatory result. Not redone.

## 3. GA aircraft

14. **Diamond DA 40 rear door, Dutch Safety Board 2011051, PH-TIN [D-ag, B1].**
    - p. 5: "The door handle operates two locking pins … The aft movement of the front locking pin operates a micro switch for a
      `door open' alert message on the primary flight display." So: **one switch, on the travel of one of two pins, at the door side.**
    - p. 9: no damage marks on either fuselage guide plate. The Board concluded "the two locking pins … were not in the guide plates"
      when the door separated.
    - pp. 2, 10: the pilot saw the 'door open' alert only **13 minutes after take-off**, seconds before the door left. "It has not been
      investigated why it took thirteen minutes before a `door open' alert was displayed … the alert should have been displayed already
      in the beginning of the flight."
    - [Y] This is a **false "locked" for 13 minutes** with pins out of their receivers. A switch that reads one pin's travel inside the
      door cannot prove the pin is in the fuselage plate. It also has no second pin channel.
15. **Diamond DA 40 NG, AAIB G-LDGB [D-ag, B2].** p. 3: "There is a DOOR OPEN warning that activates on the PFD if the primary locking
    mechanism is not properly located. However, there is no way of demonstrating this warning on the PFD without opening the doors."
    p. 2: the instructor described "the most common occurrence being door warnings and how these can be triggered by the door handle
    being open a small amount", then pushed the handle in flight to show how to secure it; the door opened and detached.
    - [Y] Again: frequent nuisance warnings teach people to "fix" the door in flight. Combined with Fokker (item 8), this argues for
      (a) high integrity against false alarms in flight and (b) a "do not touch, land" procedure.
16. **DA 40 front canopy [D-ag, B4, already in R0].** A commenter on AD 2010-25-01 asked to remove "the front canopy latch sensor" from the
    shared door-open annunciation. FAA declined. [Y] DA 40 = one canopy latch sensor + one rear-door pin switch, on one light.
17. **DA 42 [Y].** TSIB Singapore N181CW (snippet only, report not obtained): "The front locking bolt would activate a door closed
    microswitch which would remove any 'Door Open' warning on the PFD." Same one-bolt, one-switch pattern as the DA 40.
18. **Cirrus SR22 G7, AFM P/N 44765-001 [D-ag, B3].** "Door Open" is an abnormal procedure (AFM p. 3A-9): abort on the take-off roll;
    in flight, maintain control and land; "The doors on the airplane will remain 1-3 inches open in flight if not latched." A text search
    of all 304 pages found **no CAS message for a door** (CAS lists at Warning/Caution/Advisory sections). [Y] The AFM treats door
    opening as pilot-detected. Whether a door sensor exists is not stated (see NOT VERIFIED).
19. **Piper, Mooney, DA 62, glider canopy-lock sensors:** nothing first-party found this session. Glider cases are in R7/R8.

## 4. Military and trainer canopies

20. **HAL patent IN 2358/CHE/2013 (fighter/trainer canopy) [D-ag, P5, as rendered by the patent site].** Five lock pins on one
    connecting rod engage brackets under the canopy. A torsion-spring "stopper" blocks locking unless the canopy is fully closed.
    "Pulling any one of the three levers from lock / forward position will first actuate a micro-switch": that switch deflates the
    canopy seal, lights a red **"CNPY"** caption and sounds an audio warning. The locked state is shown by a colour slot on the internal
    lever (all green = locked, any red = not). "The system requires safety features which ensures partial locking is not stable."
    - [Y] The electrical sensing is on the **levers**, not on the five pins. Integrity comes from mechanism design (no stable partial lock).
21. **Aero L-39** (microswitches in the lock mechanism; SAFO 13003 separations **with** a CANOPY LOCKED light): in R7/R8. Not redone.
22. **BAC Lightning T5 [Y].** A flight-simulator site that cites RAF APs lists an auxiliary warning caption "CAN – Canopy unlocked",
    orange, which does **not** trigger the attention-getters or the audio. Secondary. Shows an old design where the canopy caption had
    no attention-getter.
23. **T-6A/B, PC-7/9/21, Tucano, G 120TP, T-38, F-16, Hawk, Yak-130:** no primary source was readable. T-6 study cards (Brainscape)
    say a red CANOPY warning and MASTER CAUTION show an unlocked canopy [Y, unverified]. Boeing patent P8 (2021) senses canopy
    *jettison angle*, not lock state [D-ag].

## 6. Bare electrical contacts in vibration and dirt (for the bolt-contact idea)

24. **Fretting [D-ag, C1 p. 2].** "The motion of the contact interface during its service life is the single most important cause of
    failure of tin-plated contacts." Fretting amplitude "generally falls in the 10 to 200 micrometer range" and comes from vibration or
    differential thermal expansion. Test: "clean tin-plated contacts with initial resistance of 1 milliohm developed a resistance in excess
    of 1 ohm in less than 20 minutes" at 10 cycles per minute; lubricated tin and gold showed no change.
25. **Contact force and stability [D-ag, C1 pp. 2–3, 5].** "the most important factor is to attain mechanical stability (i.e., the
    prevention of motion at the contact interface)". As force approaches "the minimum recommended level of 100 grams", lubrication is
    needed. Two or more spaced contacts improve stability. Tin "should not be used to make or break currents". Tin-to-gold mating is
    prone to fretting failure; lubricants help less there (p. 5).
26. **Minimum load for switch contacts [D-ag, C2 p. 3, contact specification table].** Omron SS: silver rivet contacts need a minimum
    of **160 mA at 5 VDC**; gold-alloy crossbar contacts **1 mA at 5 VDC**. The minimum is an "N standard reference value" for a failure
    rate of 1/2,000,000 operations at 60 % reliability (λ60, JIS C5003). A "micro load" operating chart (p. 7) marks the region below
    the minimum as **"Inoperable range"**. These are sealed, wiping, snap-action contacts; an open steel-on-steel joint is worse [Y].
27. **AC 43.13-1B [already R4, D-ag]:** do not use silver contacts below 8 V or 0.5 A unless rated for low energy.
28. **Bonding and moving joints [D-ag, C3 §§ 11-187–11-189, pp. 11-73 to 11-75].** Exterior parts may bond through "mechanical joints,
    conductive hinges, or bond straps" (for static and lightning only). Bonding jumpers must not exceed **0.003 ohm** per connection.
    "One of the more frequent causes of failures in electrical system bonding and grounding is corrosion." Inspection: where arcing is
    seen, "check for intermittent electrical contact between conducting surfaces". [Y] Aircraft practice accepts metal-to-metal joints for
    static/lightning bonding, not as a signal path that must read reliably at low current.
29. **Railway track circuits, the closest "moving steel part completes the circuit" precedent [D-ag, C4].** A train's wheels and axle
    short the two rails to show "occupied". RSSB: "Wheel/rail contamination can electrically insulate a train's wheels from the rail."
    "This can fail to 'short' a track circuit so that it appears that no train is present." These are wrong-side track circuit failures
    (WSTCFs). [Y] This is with tonnes of contact force. Note the polarity: in track circuits lost contact = unsafe; in the team's
    design lost contact = alarm (safe side, but nuisance).
30. **Stainless steel as a contact [Y].** Search snippets (patents US 12049688, US 9728880; an EPJ AP paper whose page returned 403)
    say the chromium passive film is non-conductive and gives two states, high and low resistance, at light load. Not read at source.

## 8. Design implications [Y] (R9 interpretation)

1. **The chosen architecture matches the post-1989 rule and current products.** "Monitor … directly" (A2), "each latch … each lock"
   (A1 §13), sensing the retained element (P2), and Honeywell's door "locked status" inductive sensor (H1) all point to **one sensor per
   bolt receiver** plus a **separate closed/seated channel** (P4). The three field cases that show false "locked" all sensed the drive or
   one pin's travel: PH-TIN (B1), Embraer (A3), UAL811/L-39 (R7/R8).
2. **Put the target where only a fully inserted bolt can be.** P2 warns against one sensor seeing two targets. Use one sensor, one target
   (the bolt tip) at full depth in each receiver.
3. **Dormant false-locked is the failure to design out.** Embraer needed a 12-month functional test (A3). Use line monitoring (NAMUR or
   IHM-style three-state) and a power-up self-test, and put a functional test (bolt out → alarm) in the pre-flight check.
4. **False alarms in flight are hazardous, not only annoying.** Fokker 100 (A4) and G-LDGB (B2) are cases where a warning led someone to
   handle the door in flight and it opened. Requirements: (a) debounce and voting so vibration cannot flicker the alarm; (b) the in-flight
   procedure says "do not touch the handle; slow down; land" (matches R8's re-latch finding).
5. **Two channels are normal.** Airbus P1 uses two sensors per door and trips to "unsafe" on disagreement. The team's "per-receiver +
   seated" gives a similar check: seated without all bolts = "not locked"; bolts "in" without seated = sensor fault.

### Verdict on the bare-contact bolt idea

What it gets right:
- **Location.** A bolt that must pass a ring and touch a plate at full depth proves "in the receiver and fully home". That is the right
  place (items 14, 20, P2, P3). It would catch PH-TIN and L-39-type cases that handle or drive sensing misses.
- **Polarity.** Open circuit = alarm, so a broken wire or lost contact fails to the alarm side.

What goes wrong — **without resistors** (plain continuity):
- **Fretting and films.** The bolt is a sliding fit with radial clearance. It touches the ring only on one side, with a force set by
  gravity and g-load, not by a spring. That is the "relative motion" case that C1 calls the single most important failure cause: 1 mΩ to
  > 1 Ω in < 20 min for clean tin. Steel or stainless steel with oxide is worse than tin [Y, C2 minimum-load logic; item 30 unverified].
  Expect **intermittent opens in flight**, especially in aerobatics and at Extra propeller frequencies (R7 haptic: 3P ≈ 120–135 Hz).
  With open = alarm, that means **in-flight nuisance alarms**, the hazard in implication 4.
- **No end-stop force at the plate.** The bolt's travel is set by the handle linkage, not by the plate. A plate the bolt only just
  reaches will read open on tolerance and thermal growth. A sprung plate fixes this, but then it is a home-made, unsealed limit switch.
- **Cannot see a short.** As R7 showed for switch-to-ground inputs, a chafed sense wire touching the source wire or structure reads
  "locked". This is a dangerous, dormant failure.
- **Bridging.** Water with dirt, de-icing or cleaning fluid, metal debris or a bent ring can connect ring to plate with no bolt. That
  reads "locked". An inductive sensor ignores water and plastic debris (H1 "non-contact … dirty, harsh environment").
- **Sneak paths [Y].** All bolts are joined by the metal linkage. If plates are wired in parallel, **one** fully home bolt closes the
  loop for all. Plates must be read as separate channels or wired strictly in series.
- **Galvanic corrosion** between the steel bolt and a copper/brass/gold plate in a wet bore (C3: corrosion is "one of the more frequent
  causes" of bonding failures).

What changes — **with series/parallel resistors** (end-of-line supervision, e.g. a series resistor at the plate and a parallel resistor
across the contact):
- **Gains:** a hard short now reads below the "closed" window and an open wire reads above the "open" window. Both become a "fault" →
  alarm. This removes the dormant short-circuit false-locked mode. That is the main benefit, and it is real.
- **Does not fix:** the contact itself. Fretting and oxide films give **any** resistance from milliohms to kilohms. To avoid nuisance
  alarms the "closed" window must be wide. A wide window lets a damp, dirty bridge (hundreds of ohms to kilohms) read "closed".
  So the resistors trade one false-locked mode (hard short) for a harder tuning problem (window width vs. film and bridge resistance).
- **New parts in a bad place:** resistors must sit at the receiver (in the bore area) to supervise the last metre of wire. They must be
  potted against water and vibration. That makes each receiver a small custom sensor anyway.

**Bottom line [Y].** No aerospace precedent was found for using the locking bolt itself as the conductor. The closest field precedent
(railway track circuits) has a known contamination failure even with very high contact force (C4). Contact physics (C1, C2) predicts
intermittent opens under vibration (nuisance alarms in flight) and the geometry allows bridging (false locked). With resistors the idea
becomes acceptable for a **bench demonstrator or a teaching contrast** in the pitch, not for flight. Keep the team's choice: an inductive
proximity sensor with NAMUR/three-state line monitoring at **the same place** (bolt tip at full depth in each receiver). If a contact
solution is wanted for cost, use a sealed, gold-contact, plunger-type switch whose plunger is pressed by the bolt tip (this is the
Eurocopter "movable pin presses a contact" pattern, P7) with end-of-line resistors. Do not let the bolt carry the signal current.
