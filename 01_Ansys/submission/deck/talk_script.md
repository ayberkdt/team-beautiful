## Likely jury questions

Answers use only facts from the submitted report. Any team member can answer.

**Q1. Aluminium is the lightest candidate. Why did you remove it?**

At 250–300 °C it has no temperature margin. This is true for the card limit (> 300 °C) and for the Granta limit (≥ 300 °C). It also fails on oxidation, stress-corrosion cracking and sourcing risk. Against steel bolts it is the anode of a galvanic couple: −0.78 to −0.70 V against −0.51 to −0.43 V. *(Report §2, Table 2)*

**Q2. Your own FE code gives 142 MPa, Ansys gives 78.5 MPa. Why the difference?**

The values come from two different models. Our own FE uses the 45 % layout and reads the stress away from the bolt bores. Ansys uses the smoothed final geometry and gives the maximum over the whole part. Both values are below the allowable stress of 345 MPa. *(Report §3, Table 3 and its caption)*

**Q3. Ansys used room-temperature properties. Is the part safe at 300 °C?**

Yes, on the strength side. The allowable stress is the yield strength at 300 °C divided by 1.5. In the linear model the stress does not change with the modulus. The displacement increases with the modulus ratio to 0.36–0.38 mm at 300 °C. The limit is 1.65 mm. *(Report §3)*

**Q4. The titanium part costs much more. Is it worth it?**

The raw material costs about 950 USD against 196 USD for steel. Laser powder-bed printing costs about 1 350 USD per kg of finished part (Ti-6Al-4V data). This gives about 52 000 USD per bracket. That is about 1 360 USD per tonne of CO₂ avoided in year one. This value decreases with each year in service. *(Report §4; references [2], [6])*

**Q5. What about fatigue, porosity and certification?**

We did not model fatigue at 300 °C, the porosity and rough surface of printed titanium, or thermal stress. Our next step is a printed prototype. We close its porosity by hot isostatic pressing and do a fatigue test at 300 °C. Then we agree the qualification route with the authority. We must also confirm the supply of Ti-8Al-1Mo-1V powder. *(Report §3, §4)*
