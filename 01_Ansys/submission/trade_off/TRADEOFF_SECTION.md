## 3. Material selection and trade-off

### 3.1 Screening against the card

The three candidates supplied by the organisers were checked against the card's material requirements using the
organisers' Granta EduPack data [1] (Fig. X1). All three pass the castability/3-D-printability screen and the
fracture-toughness floor of 10 MPa·m½. **Al 2618-T6 is eliminated** on the working-temperature requirement: its
maximum service temperature is 250–300 °C, so it does not exceed 300 °C as the card demands [1, p.9; 3]. The
organisers' own Granta filter used "≥ 300 °C" [1, p.4], which is why the alloy appears in the shortlist; we apply the
card's stricter wording and flag the difference. Four further properties confirm the decision: there is no design
strength data for 2618 above 204 °C, where the T6 temper over-ages [5]; oxidation resistance at 500 °C is rated
*unacceptable*, stress-corrosion cracking *highly susceptible*, and sourcing risk *very high* [1, p.10–11]. S275N is
the reference; **Ti-8Al-1Mo-1V is the only candidate that meets every requirement**.

### 3.2 Why a material that is ten times dirtier to make wins over the life cycle

Per kilogram, Ti-8Al-1Mo-1V costs 23–26 USD and emits 42 kg CO₂-eq in production (virgin grade + powder forming),
against 1.0–1.6 USD and 9 kg CO₂-eq for S275N [1, p.8, 11–12]. The card's use scenario, however, charges every
kilogram of bracket 720 000 km of long-haul flight: 0.000468 kg CO₂ per kg·km, i.e. **337 kg CO₂-eq per kilogram of
part** [3]. With the organisers' phase formulas the life-cycle footprint becomes 346 kg CO₂/kg for S275N and 379
kg CO₂/kg for Ti-8Al-1Mo-1V; the use phase is 97 % and 89 % of it. The material choice therefore changes the
footprint per kilogram by only 10 %, while the mass changes it in direct proportion. Our model reproduces the
organisers' initial-design eco audit (52.6 t CO₂-eq for the 152 kg part [2]) within 1 % in every phase.

Three break-even numbers follow (Fig. X3a):

* **Same shape, new material.** The unchanged geometry in Ti weighs 84.9 kg and emits 32.2 t (−39 %) — before any
  optimisation.
* **Ti is better than the initial part up to 164 % of its volume** (139 kg): there is no Ti design that loses to
  the current steel bracket on CO₂.
* **Against an optimised steel part**, steel ties with Ti at **0.61 × the Ti volume**. Example ⟨45 %⟩: the Ti design
  emits 14.5 t; a steel design would have to reach 27 % of the initial volume (42 kg) to match it.

### 3.3 Is 0.61 × volume reachable in steel? Performance indices at 300 °C

Whether steel can reach that volume depends on what limits the design. We used Ashby's minimum-mass indices [4]
with the allowable stress (σy/1.5) and modulus **at 300 °C** (S275N: 107 MPa, 168 GPa; Ti-8Al-1Mo-1V: 345 MPa,
108 GPa; knock-downs from EN 1993-1-2 Annex E and Table 3.1 [6] and MIL-HDBK-5J [5]) and multiplied by the life-cycle CO₂ per kilogram
(Fig. X2):

| Governing mode | Index (mass ∝) | Ti CO₂ / S275N CO₂ |
|---|---|---|
| Tie, strength-limited | ρ/σ | 0.19 (−81 %) |
| Beam, strength-limited | ρ/σ^2/3 | 0.28 (−72 %) |
| Beam, stiffness-limited | ρ/E^1/2 | 0.76 (−24 %) |
| Tie, stiffness-limited | ρ/E | 0.95 (−5 %) |

**Insight.** Titanium's advantage comes from its strength at temperature (3.2× the steel allowable), not from its
stiffness (specific stiffness is almost equal, [1, p.8]). Where strength governs, a steel bracket needs 3–6× more
mass than titanium and cannot reach 0.61 × the Ti volume. Where the 1.65 mm displacement limit governs, an optimised
steel bracket would come within 5–24 % of titanium. The topology optimisation (Section 4) therefore has to push the
Ti design until the displacement limit becomes active; we report which constraint governs the final design.

### 3.4 Cost

Using the organisers' price only (material cost = mass × price; no manufacturing cost data were supplied), the
same-shape Ti part costs 2 087 USD (10.9× the 192 USD steel part); the ⟨45 %⟩ design costs 939 USD (4.9×) — **+747
USD for 38 t CO₂-eq avoided, i.e. about 20 USD per tonne avoided** (Fig. X3b). Every kilogram removed by the topology
optimisation saves 25 USD of titanium and 379 kg CO₂-eq, so the optimisation improves both objectives at once.

### 3.5 Analytic relations

Every phase prescribed by the card is proportional to the part mass m, so the life-cycle (first-life) footprint is a
straight line through the origin, C = c·m, with c the sum of the phase factors per kilogram [1–3]:

| Phase (kg CO₂-eq per kg of part) | S275N | Ti-8Al-1Mo-1V |
|---|---:|---:|
| Material, virgin grade | 3.03 | 35.31 |
| Manufacture, metal powder forming | 5.99 | 6.73 |
| Transport, 500 km × 0.241 kg/(t·km) | 0.12 | 0.12 |
| Use, 720 000 km × 0.000468 kg/(kg·km) | 336.96 | 336.96 |
| Disposal | 0.049 | 0.049 |
| **c, life-cycle (first life)** | **346.1** | **379.2** |

With m in kg and C in t CO₂-eq:

- (1) C_S = 0.3461 · m_S  and  C_Ti = 0.3792 · m_Ti  (Al 2618-T6: C_Al = 0.3542 · m_Al)
- (2) Equal CO₂: **m_S = 1.095 · m_Ti**, i.e. m_Ti = 0.913 · m_S — one kilogram of titanium "costs" as much
  CO₂ over the life as 1.10 kg of steel.
- (3) In volume (f = % of the initial 19.4 L design): C_S = 0.5271 · f_S and C_Ti = 0.3218 · f_Ti; equal CO₂ when
  **f_S = 0.610 · f_Ti** (f_Ti = 1.638 · f_S).
- (4) Same geometry: m_Ti = 0.5573 · m_S (density ratio).
- (5) Saving against the initial part (152.27 kg, 52.71 t): **ΔC = 52.71 − 0.3792 · m_Ti**, or
  ΔC/C₀ = 1 − m_Ti / 139.0; titanium breaks even with the initial part at m_Ti = 139.0 kg.
- (6) Material cost (price × mass): K_S = 1.261 · m_S, K_Ti = 24.59 · m_Ti; extra cost against the
  initial part **ΔK = 24.59 · m_Ti − 192.1** USD (cost parity only below m_Ti = 7.8 kg).
- (7) Cost of carbon avoided: **ΔK/ΔC = (24.59 · m_Ti − 192.1) / (52.71 − 0.3792 · m_Ti)** USD per t.

Including the end-of-life recycling credit [2] the slopes become 343.9 (S275N) and 354.1
(Ti-8Al-1Mo-1V) kg/kg, and (2) becomes m_S = 1.030 · m_Ti: the titanium penalty per kilogram shrinks
from 9.5 % to 3.0 %.
