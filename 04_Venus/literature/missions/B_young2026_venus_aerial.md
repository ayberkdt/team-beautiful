# B_young2026_venus_aerial — NASA Ames: Venus aerial explorers with emphasis on rotary-wing

## What I read
Full text (pypdf). Figures (mission profiles, fold sequences, CFD) are images: only captions read.

## Content / numbers [D-ag]
- Three flight regimes: **above the clouds (>60 km)**, **below the clouds (<50 km)**, **near/on surface (<10 km)**, plus "dive or cycle" vehicles (p. 4).
- Key statement for ATC flyers: "The biggest technical challenge ... is the folding and deploying of aircraft from their stowed state in entry aeroshells" to controlled level flight (p. 4); mission profile = entry → mid-air deployment → **dive and pullout** to level flight (Fig. 3, Fig. 6).
- "Experience for successful deployment of aircraft from mid-air at high altitudes has been limited to aircraft with **two to four wing folds**" (refs: Ames Mars airplane balloon drops; ARES half-scale balloon drop) (p. 5).
- Propeller blades: "**Spring-loaded mechanisms** might be required to unfold the blades prior to spinning them to avoid the blades clipping the wing surface" (p. 5).
- Aeroelastic stability during dive/pullout after aeroshell release flagged as secondary challenge; discrete hinges reduce stiffness (p. 6).
- Below-cloud concept: re-purposed ARES Mars airplane with external **MMRTG** (~100 W nominal; insufficient ΔT at Venus for sustained flight; used for bursts) at 25–50 km (p. 6–7).
- Near-surface rotorcraft: twin pressurized hulls with tandem tilting wings/propellers (Young & Aiken 2001 ERF); stopped **cycloidal rotor** VTOL (Husseyin 2016, NTRS 20160012563); mission limited to "a few hours" by electronics heat death (p. 8).
- **Rotary-wing decelerators (RWD)** for probe descent (Young et al. 2004, IPPW-2, NASA CP-2004-213456) and **samara-seed** probe (Cooney 2013 "MiSSiVE"); hybrid **lifting-body autogyro (LBA)** that bypasses deployment from an aeroshell by integrating the rotors on the entry body; descent angle of attack ~**35°** (Fig. 16); rotor deployment sequence: rotors stowed along upper surface → arms partly deployed, non-rotating → spinning, fully deployed autogyro (Fig. 18) (p. 9–11).
- "Petey" stopped-rotor concept for altitude cycling; falcon-stoop bio-inspired diver (p. 11–12).
- Table 3 challenge #1: "New entry, deployment, release (and dive/pullout) approaches compatible with each class of Venus aerial vehicle" (p. 13).
- Atmosphere table (Table 2, p. 3): Venus surface 735 K, 65 kg/m³, 9.2×10⁶ Pa, speed of sound 410 m/s, γ 1.286, g 8.9 m/s².

## Relevance
This is the most directly relevant precedent document for a Venus rotorcraft: NASA Ames (Ingenuity/Dragonfly rotorcraft group) explicitly frames the deployment problem and offers three architectures: (a) fold & mid-air unfold from aeroshell then dive/pullout, (b) rotary-wing decelerator spun up during descent, (c) entry body with integrated rotors (LBA) — no aeroshell separation.

## Caveats
- Conceptual paper; no masses, no deployment altitudes or timings given.

## Quote
"rotary-wing flight near the surface should not be abandoned" (p. 8).
